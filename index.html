Read a year of transcripts at once.

After twelve months you remember session 1, session 9 — the big one — and last week. Everything in between has collapsed into an impression. Thera.py restores the middle: it counts the things in a transcript that are too small to hear one at a time, plots them across the whole course, and makes every number clickable back to the lines that produced it.

It runs entirely inside your browser. Nothing is uploaded, and you can check that yourself in thirty seconds — see below.

What you get

Four views, and a way back into the text from every number in all of them.

Ingest report
What was actually read, per file: format, language, speaker labels, whether there are timestamps — and which figures are therefore unavailable. It comes first on purpose. Whoever sees curves before they learn the speakers were guessed has already believed the curves.
Session card
One page per hour: the language markers with their confidence grades, affect through the session, talk ratio, question types, vocabulary appearing for the first time, and threads that were dropped.
Arc
The year at once. Every marker as a trajectory with Bayesian changepoints, a sociogram of who enters and leaves the narrative, and what distinguishes this client from the rest of your caseload.
Mirror
The uncomfortable one, and the most useful. Your intervention profile per client, the comparison between clients, and your own recurring formulas. Therapists are trained on their own process and almost never get quantitative feedback on it.

Why nothing is uploaded here

This page is a set of static files. When you drop a transcript on it, the browser reads it into memory and analyses it there, with a Python runtime that also runs inside the browser. There is no place in this program that sends transcript content anywhere.

You do not have to take my word for it — you can check: load this page once, then switch off your Wi-Fi or unplug the network cable, and keep working. Everything still works. An application that transmitted your data could not do that.

The flip side, so that it is said: results live only in this browser tab. Reload the page and you start over. Anything you want to keep, you export yourself. In exchange, Thera.py cannot be subpoenaed, breached, or quietly changed under you.

What this tool does not do

No diagnoses, no scores, no severity ratings, and no “insights” written in sentences. It counts things, plots them over time, and makes every number clickable back to the line of transcript that produced it. The interpretation is your work and stays your work.

Two languages, two rulers

Thera.py reads German and English sessions, each with its own word lists. It never translates: the markers are properties of a language, not of a text, and a translated lexicon would measure the translation. German is counted for its man/ich ratio, Konjunktiv II density and modal particles like halt and eben; English for generic you, would have and I wish, and downtoners like just and anyway. Same idea, different machinery — and every example quoted back to you stays in the language it was spoken in.

What follows from that, and it matters: within one language the numbers compare cleanly. Across the two they do not — the word lists are different sizes, so the levels sit differently for reasons that have nothing to do with the client. The language is detected per file and shown in the ingest report, where you can correct it in one click; and anywhere the tool puts the two side by side, it says so.

What the numbers can carry

Every figure in the interface carries a confidence grade and a plain note about what it can bear. A number without a statement of its limits is not information, it is a claim.

A
Well grounded, robustly computable.
A*
Well grounded — but only if the transcript has timestamps.
B
Sound reasoning, heuristic implementation. Useful as a trajectory.
C
Exploratory. Interesting to look at, not to conclude from.

And, without softening: one session is noise — below roughly ten sessions most markers mean nothing. A transcript is not a session; tone, pause, body and silence are gone. Speech-recognition errors are not random, and they fall hardest on exactly the low-frequency emotional vocabulary the granularity measure most wants to count. The lexicons are adapted, not validated.

Consent is yours to settle. Your clients consented to being recorded. They almost certainly did not consent to computational analysis of their language. That is your call to make — but it should be made deliberately rather than by default, and it is worth raising with your professional body.

Who are these people?

Thera.py has guessed at personal names in the text. You know which of these are actually people; the machine does not. Whatever you confirm is replaced by a stable placeholder (“Person A”) before anything else is computed. The mapping from placeholder to name stays in your browser and is never stored.
